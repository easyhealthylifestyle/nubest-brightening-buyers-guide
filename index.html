Read the label before the reviews

Skin brightening supplements range from carefully tested formulas to products with vague labels and big promises. In the U.S., the FDA does not approve dietary supplements before they go on sale, so the label is your best first filter. This guide shows what to look for.

    Ingredients you’ll see often

    Evidence for oral skin supplements varies by ingredient and often comes from small, short studies. Treat bold brightening claims with care, and use this list to understand what you are reading.

    Glutathione
    A compound the body makes from three amino acids. It works as an antioxidant and is the headline ingredient in many brightening formulas.
    Vitamin C
    An essential vitamin the body needs to make collagen, a structural protein in skin. Also an antioxidant.
    Vitamin E
    A fat-soluble antioxidant, often paired with vitamin C.
    Alpha lipoic acid
    An antioxidant compound the body makes in small amounts.
    Collagen peptides
    Collagen broken down into shorter protein chains so it dissolves easily.
    Hyaluronic acid
    A substance found naturally in skin that holds water. More common in topical products, but also sold in capsules.
    Niacinamide (vitamin B3)
    A form of vitamin B3. Widely used in topical skin care; also found in some oral formulas.

    Habits that matter more than any capsule

    • Sunscreen every day. Broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher, reapplied about every two hours outdoors. Sun exposure is a major driver of uneven-looking skin.
    • Colorful, varied meals. Fruit, vegetables, protein and healthy fats supply the same nutrients many skin supplements contain.
    • Enough sleep. Most adults need seven or more hours a night.
    • A gentle, steady routine. Introduce one new product at a time and patch test before using it on your face.
    • A dermatologist for persistent concerns. Dark patches, melasma-like marks or sudden changes in skin deserve a professional look.

    A note on injections

    Capsules and injections are not the same thing. The U.S. FDA has not approved any injectable product for changing skin tone and has cautioned consumers about injectable skin products sold online or at spas, which can contain glutathione, vitamin C or other ingredients. If a product or clinic offers “glow drips” or shots, talk with a board-certified dermatologist first.
